Purpose. Set a warm, serious tone and signal that this workshop is about character, not rules.

Say. "Good morning, everyone. Over the next two hours we will talk about what it really means to wear the prefect badge, how to make fair decisions, and how to keep your studies and your health in balance. Nobody here is being tested. This is a conversation among leaders."

Do. Stand at the front, greet the board, and mention the plan briefly: discussion, two group activities, and a short break. Agree on simple ground rules: listen, respect, phones away.

Timing. 1 minute. Total session 120 minutes.

Transition. "Let us start with one question I want you to think about before I say anything else."

The central question

“What makes someone a leader?”

The badge

A badge gives you a position.

The leader

Your actions determine what that position means.

Facilitator: invite two or three students to share their opinion.

Purpose. Surface the students' existing beliefs about leadership before teaching anything.

Say. "Think about a leader you respect: a teacher, a senior prefect from your school, a cricket captain, a family member. What made you follow them? Was it the title?"

Ask. Take two or three volunteers. If the room is quiet, ask students to turn to a neighbour for 30 seconds first.

Expect. Answers such as "respect", "fairness", "listens to us", "gives a good example". Some will say "power" or "discipline". Accept all answers and write keywords on the board.

Teaching point. The badge is given by the school. What it means is decided by your daily behaviour.

Timing. 3 minutes.

Transition. "Let us see how this plays out in a very real situation from a school day."

The school scenario

“The principal is absent. Students are gathering noisily during interval.”

What should a prefect do?

Discuss with the person next to you: what is the first action you would take?

  • Check that everyone is safe first.
  • Stay calm. Do not shout.
  • Guide, explain, then follow up.
  • Involve a teacher if it escalates.

Purpose. Anchor the workshop in a familiar situation and let students feel the pull of different possible responses.

Say. "Picture it: it is interval, the principal is away, the corridor outside the library is packed and the noise is rising. Teachers are in the staffroom. Everyone is looking at the prefects. What do you do first?"

Ask. "What is your very first action?" Give 60 seconds for pair discussion, then take three answers.

Expect. Some will say "blow the whistle and scold them" or "send them to class". Others will say "check if anyone is hurt" or "ask them calmly". Praise safety-first and calm responses. Do not shame the shouting answer. Ask, "What would happen to trust if we do that every day?"

Teaching point. Responses range from control to leadership. We will build a toolkit during the day.

Transition. "Let us begin by defining what leadership actually is."

What is leadership?

Leadership is the ability to influence, guide, and inspire people towards a common goal while taking responsibility for decisions and actions.
  • Influence
  • Direction
  • Service
  • Accountability

Purpose. Offer a working definition that students can remember through four anchor words.

Say. "Read the definition with me. Notice what is missing: it does not mention rank, age, or punishment. It talks about influence, direction, service and accountability. We will unpack each of these in a moment."

Ask. "Which of the four words is hardest for a new prefect?" Many will say accountability, because it means owning mistakes in front of friends.

Teaching point. Leadership is something you do, not something you have.

Timing. 3 minutes.

Purpose. Contrast surface authority with real leadership so students can self-assess.

Say. "On the left is what the badge gives you. On the right is what leadership earns. A junior may stand in line because you are a prefect. Whether they respect you is another matter."

Ask. "Think of someone who led through fear. Did people do their best work for them, or only when they were watching?"

Example. A prefect at the school gate who shouts at latecomers may get silence, but a prefect who greets them and explains why punctuality matters earns cooperation next week too.

Teaching point. Authority gets attention. Integrity gets trust.

Purpose. Turn the four anchor words into practical meanings.

Say. "Influence: people copy what you do. Direction: people know what we are trying to achieve, whether it is a tidy assembly or a smooth sports meet. Service: you are there to help, not to be served. Accountability: when something goes wrong, you say 'that was my responsibility' before anyone has to ask."

Ask. "Give me an example of service from a prefect you know at school." Examples: staying back to arrange chairs after assembly, helping a Grade 6 student find a classroom.

Teaching point. A leader who lacks any of the four will have a gap: example without direction is confusing, direction without service feels bossy.

Timing. 2 minutes.

Transition. "Influence starts with something very simple: your own behaviour."

Lead by example

Your behaviour teaches even when you are not speaking.

  • Follow school rules before enforcing them.
  • Arrive on time.
  • Speak respectfully.
  • Complete assigned duties.
  • Admit mistakes.

Purpose. Make leadership concrete through five visible habits.

Say. "Juniors are always watching. If a prefect tells them to tuck in their shirt while their own is untucked, the lesson they learn is 'rules are for others'. If a prefect arrives late to a duty, the duty roster stops meaning anything."

Ask. "Which of the five is the easiest to forget when you are tired or in a hurry?" Many will say arriving on time or speaking respectfully when stressed.

Important point. Admitting mistakes does not weaken you. A prefect who says 'sorry, I got that wrong' gains credibility.

Timing. 2 minutes.

Transition. "Beyond habits, what qualities should an effective prefect build?"

Purpose. Introduce the five qualities that the rest of Section B explores.

Say. "These five qualities are not talents you are born with. They are habits you can practise. We will spend time on integrity, empathy, communication and teamwork now, and self-discipline will return when we talk about time and energy."

Activity (optional). Ask each student to silently rate themselves from 1 to 5 on each quality and circle the one they most want to grow. Tell them they will not be asked to share the scores.

Ask. "Which quality do you think the school community notices first in a prefect?" Likely answers: fairness, or communication.

Timing. 4 minutes. Move through each quality in about 30 to 40 seconds and leave the rest for the self-rating.

Transition. "Let us test the first one with a real dilemma."

Integrity: doing the right thing

“Your close friend breaks a school rule and asks you to ignore it because you are a prefect.”

What does fairness require?

  • The same rule applies to everyone.
  • Speak privately and respectfully.
  • Correct the behaviour, not the person.

Purpose. Present a realistic loyalty-versus-fairness dilemma without giving the answer first.

Say. "Imagine your best friend from Grade 8 has left the school premises during interval without permission, or is using a phone in class. He looks at you and says, 'Machan, you are a prefect, just let it go.' What now?"

Ask. "What does fairness require? What would you say to your friend? What would happen to other students' trust if you looked away?" Let three or four students respond before revealing the prompts.

Expected responses. "Ignore it because he is my friend" (acknowledge the pressure honestly), "report him" (ask whether a private conversation first is appropriate for a minor matter), "warn him this time" (ask whether others would get a warning too).

Teaching points. Consistent and respectful enforcement. No favouritism and no unnecessary humiliation. Speak privately, explain the rule, apply the same response that anyone else would receive. Serious or repeated matters go to a teacher. Being fair to a friend can be an act of friendship.

Timing. 3 minutes.

Transition. "Fairness does not mean being harsh. That brings us to empathy."

Empathy and respectful authority

Humiliating a studentCorrecting behaviour respectfully
Listening to replyListening to understand

Empathy does not mean ignoring rules.

Purpose. Show that kindness and firmness work together.

Say. "Each row shows two ways to handle the same moment. The left side may work today, but it damages trust. The right side takes a little more effort and builds a school where students want to follow the rules."

Ask. "Think about the last time an adult corrected you. Which approach made you want to improve?" Take one or two answers.

Example. A Grade 7 student is found without a tie. Instead of calling out in front of the line, a prefect quietly says, 'Please bring it tomorrow. Do you need help getting one?' The rule remains, the dignity stays.

Key warning. Empathy is not permission. We still apply rules. We do it in a way that treats the person with respect.

Timing. 2 minutes.

Transition. "Leaders rarely work alone. Let us see how a team works together."

Purpose. Show how clear communication turns a group of prefects into a team.

Say. "Think of preparing for the inter-house sports meet. If four prefects each think someone else is organizing water and first aid, nobody does. Giving each task a name and a deadline removes confusion."

Ask. "When did a school event go wrong because nobody was sure who was responsible?" Invite a short story.

Teaching points. Clear instructions (who, what, by when), active listening (repeat it back), task ownership (one name per task), coordination (share updates), follow-up (check progress and say thank you).

Tip. A simple WhatsApp or notice-board checklist with names and times is enough. Keep it respectful and professional.

Timing. 2 minutes.

Transition. "There is one trap here: trying to do everything yourself."

Leadership is not doing everything

“If everything depends on you, you are not building a strong team.”

1

Assign the right task to the right person.

2

Clarify the expected outcome.

3

Agree on a deadline.

4

Follow up without micromanaging.

Purpose. Introduce delegation as a leadership skill, not a way to avoid work.

Say. "Some of the strongest prefects burn out because they cannot let go. Delegation is not laziness. It is how you grow other people and keep yourself able to lead."

Walk through. Right person for the task (a student with a clear voice for announcements). Clear outcome ("a tidy hall by 7:30"). A deadline. Follow-up: check once, offer help, avoid hovering.

Ask. "What is the difference between following up and micromanaging?" Expected: asking 'how is it going, do you need anything?' versus redoing the task or checking every five minutes.

Teaching point. A strong team can function even when you are absent for an exam week.

Timing. 2 minutes.

Transition. "Now a harder dilemma, where the person causing a problem has more authority than you."

Leadership dilemma: the senior prefect

“A senior prefect humiliates a junior student. You believe the behaviour is wrong, but the senior prefect has more authority than you.”

What would responsible leadership look like?

  1. Support the junior student right away.
  2. Raise your concern respectfully, in private.
  3. Seek support from a trusted teacher or the prefect master.
  4. Report serious misconduct to a teacher.
SeniorYouJunior

Purpose. Explore courage and responsibility when authority is unequal.

Say. "This is difficult. You may fear being labelled disrespectful or losing your friends. Yet if you stay silent, the junior learns that nobody will protect them."

Ask. "What are the possible actions? What are the risks of each?" Collect options on the board: say nothing, confront publicly, speak privately, talk to a teacher.

Teaching points. First, care for the junior student: a quiet word, a check-in. Second, respectful intervention: speak to the senior prefect privately and without accusing ("That did not look right. Can we handle it differently?"). Third, seek support from a trusted teacher such as the prefect master. Fourth, serious misconduct, such as physical harm, bullying or repeated humiliation, must be reported to a teacher. This is not betrayal. It is protecting students and the integrity of the board.

Caution. Avoid public confrontation that shames the senior as well. Respect applies both ways.

Timing. 3 minutes. Hand out the groups for the next activity while concluding.

Purpose. Let students practise decisions in a low-risk setting.

Set-up (2 minutes). Form groups of four or five, mixing grades where possible. Give each group a sheet or notebook page. Assign each group all three dilemmas, or allocate one dilemma per group if the board is large, then share across groups.

Run (9 minutes of discussion). Start the timer for 15 minutes. Groups discuss, identify the conflict (for example, friendship versus fairness; respect versus rank; duty versus exam), propose a responsible action, and explain why. Walk around, listen, and prompt: "Who is affected? What is the most respectful way? When would you involve a teacher?"

Debrief (last 4 minutes). Ask two or three groups to share one decision and their reasoning. Reinforce: consistency, respect, courage, early communication. For dilemma 3, link ahead to Section D (balancing studies and duties).

Expected responses. 1: private, fair, same as anyone else. 2: support the junior, speak privately, involve a teacher if needed. 3: inform teachers early, request a swap or reduced duty, do not abandon safety-critical tasks.

Purpose. Name the real pressures honestly and validate the students' experience.

Say. "Look at everything around the word 'You'. Classes, tuition and the bus journey, revision, prefect duties, and then family and friends and rest. That is a lot for one person. If you ever feel stretched, you are not weak. The load is real."

Ask. "Which of these takes up most of your week? Which one gets squeezed first?" Common answers: sleep, family time, hobbies. Many Grade 12 and 13 prefects will mention tuition travel.

Teaching point. Our aim is not to remove demands. It is to choose wisely among them.

Transition. "So is time management the whole answer? Not quite."

The problem is not always time

“You cannot control every demand on your time. You can control how you respond to many of them.”

  • PrioritizationChoose what matters most.
  • PlanningDecide before the week begins.
  • BoundariesSay what you can and cannot do.
  • DelegationShare the load fairly.
  • CommunicationSpeak up early.

Heavy workloads, travel, and family duties are real. This is not only about personal discipline.

Purpose. Reframe time management as responding wisely, without blaming students.

Say. "Sometimes the problem is not that you wasted time. The timetable is full, the bus is slow, and a school event is moved forward. You cannot control that. What you can control is how you plan, what you prioritize, whom you ask for help, and when you speak up."

Ask. "Which of the five tools do you use already? Which do you avoid?" Many avoid communication because they fear looking weak.

Caution. Avoid telling students that poor discipline is the only reason they struggle. Acknowledge long school hours, tuition, travel, and family duties.

Timing. 3 minutes.

Transition. "Let us look at a tool that helps with prioritizing."

The four-quadrant priority matrix

UrgentNot urgent ImportantLess important

1 Do promptly

Exam tomorrow. A student who needs first aid at an event.

2 Schedule

Weekly revision. Planning next month's event. Sleep.

3 Delegate where appropriate

Collecting notices. Arranging chairs. Updating a duty list.

4 Reduce or eliminate

Endless scrolling. Long chats during duty time.

Purpose. Teach a simple, widely used prioritization tool (often called the Eisenhower matrix).

Say. "Two questions for every task: is it urgent, and is it important? Urgent means it needs attention now. Important means it matters for your goals and for other people. This gives four boxes."

Walk through. Box 1: do promptly, for example revision for tomorrow's paper or an injured student at the sports meet. Box 2: schedule, the most valuable box, such as regular revision and sleep, because it prevents Box 1 crises. Box 3: urgent but less important, such as chasing notices or arranging chairs, so delegate where appropriate. Box 4: neither, so reduce or remove.

Interactive. Call out tasks and ask students to point to the box: "Preparing for the term test next week" (2), "A friend messages about a meme" (4), "The assembly microphone is not working" (1 or 3, discuss).

Caution. Boxes depend on context. Safety always stays in Box 1.

Timing. 6 minutes.

Transition. "Let us apply it to a situation many of you will face soon."

Example: exam tomorrow, event today

“You have an examination tomorrow morning, and your prefect team needs help preparing for a school event today.”

Safety-related duties must still be handled properly, with a clear handover.

  1. Identify what is essential.
  2. Communicate the conflict early.
  3. Delegate or exchange duties.
  4. Protect a realistic revision period.
  5. Maintain adequate sleep.

Purpose. Model a responsible decision sequence for a common conflict.

Say. "Imagine the Prize Giving rehearsal is this afternoon and your Chemistry paper is tomorrow at 8. You are a key helper. Do you attend all, none, or part?"

Walk through. Identify what is essential (what only you can do?). Tell the prefect master or team lead early today, not at 9 p.m. Offer to swap or complete something beforehand. Block a realistic revision window. Sleep matters; an all-nighter is not a plan.

Ask. "What could you say to the team leader right now?" Link to Rule 3 later.

Safety note. If you hold a safety role (crowd control, first aid, gate), arrange a proper handover so that nothing is left uncovered.

Teaching point. Early communication is leadership, not an excuse.

Timing. 4 minutes.

Transition. "Now four simple rules that make this easier. Rule 1: plan the week."

Rule 1

Plan the week.

  1. 1List fixed commitments.
  2. 2Add examination dates and deadlines.
  3. 3Reserve revision periods.
  4. 4Schedule prefect duties.
  5. 5Include travel, meals, breaks, and sleep.

Purpose. Teach a repeatable weekly planning routine.

Say. "Plan on Sunday evening or Friday afternoon, for ten minutes. Start with what cannot move: school hours, tuition, prefect meetings. Add exams and deadlines. Then place revision and duties around them. Do not forget the travel time on the bus or train, meals, breaks and sleep."

Ask. "What usually goes wrong with your plans?" Likely: too ambitious, no buffer, forgetting travel time.

Tip. Leave about one free slot per day for surprises. A plan that is 100 percent full will fail by Tuesday.

Reminder. Everyone's timetable is different. Use what suits your own school, tuition and transport.

Purpose. Offer practical study habits without making promises about results.

Say. "Studying for four hours once a week is usually less effective than shorter sessions several times a week. Try a focused block, for example 25 minutes with the phone away, then a five-minute break. The numbers are only an example."

Explain. Active recall: close the book and write down or say what you remember. Practice questions: past papers and teacher-set questions. Regular revision: revisit topics after a few days so they do not fade.

Ask. "What is your biggest distraction during study?" Often phones, noise at home, or tiredness after tuition.

Caution. Do not promise marks. People learn differently, and some students need more support from teachers.

Timing. 3 minutes.

Transition. "Rule 3: what do you do when your duties and your studies collide?"

Rule 3

Delegate and communicate.

  1. Recognize the conflict
  2. Inform the relevant person
  3. Propose alternatives
  4. Confirm responsibilities
Try saying“I have an examination tomorrow. Can I exchange this duty or help complete another part earlier?”

Asking for help early is responsible behaviour.

Purpose. Give students language and a process for raising conflicts.

Say. "Many prefects stay silent until the last minute, then miss the duty or fail to prepare. Compare that with early communication: you recognize the clash, tell the prefect master or team leader, propose a solution, and confirm who will do what."

Practice. Pair students: one plays the prefect with an exam, the other plays the team leader. Two minutes, then swap. Encourage them to use the sample phrase in their own words.

Ask. "Why do we hesitate to ask?" Fear of letting people down, fear of looking weak. Respond: early notice is a gift to the team. Late notice causes problems.

Teaching point. Offering an alternative shows commitment: you are not escaping, you are solving.

Timing. 4 minutes.

Transition. "Rule 4 protects the most important resource: you."

Rule 4

Protect your energy.

“Rest is part of sustainable performance, not a reward for finishing everything.”

  • Sleep
  • Breaks
  • Realistic expectations
  • Managing stress

Purpose. Normalize rest and challenge the glorification of overwork.

Say. "Staying up until 2 a.m. does not make you a better leader or student. Tired people make poor decisions and lose patience. Sleep, breaks and stress management are part of your plan, not extras."

Ask. "What helps you recharge: a walk, cricket or football, music, time with family, prayer or quiet time?" Collect a few answers and normalize them.

Teaching points. Realistic expectations: you cannot be perfect at everything this week. If stress feels heavy, talk to a trusted adult, a counsellor, or a teacher.

Caution. Never praise sleep deprivation. Avoid jokes that celebrate overwork.

Being responsible does not mean being available for everything, all the time.

Purpose. Provide a clear strategy for the highest-pressure weeks (term tests, O/L or A/L preparation).

Say. "Six steps. Before exams: map the dates, find your hardest subjects, and tell people about conflicts early. During exams: do only the essential duties and step back from optional ones. After: return to normal duties and thank those who covered for you."

Ask. "Which duties are essential, and which could pause for a week?" Essential: safety and core duties. Optional: decorating, extra committees, non-urgent errands.

Key message. Stepping back temporarily is responsible. Good leaders recognize their limits.

The challenge

“You are a Grade 12 prefect. School runs Monday to Friday. You have tuition on three evenings, a prefect meeting on Wednesday, a school event on Friday, and an examination on Monday. You must complete homework and revise two difficult subjects.”

Your task

Build a realistic weekly schedule that protects academic progress, prefect responsibilities, and rest.

Purpose. Introduce the final practical activity.

Say. "Here is a realistic week. Read it slowly. Some details are fixed: school, three tuition evenings, a Wednesday meeting, a Friday event, and Monday's exam. Your job is to build a plan that survives real life."

Clarify. Groups can choose which evenings have tuition and whether the exam is the Monday that ends the planning week. They may invent reasonable details but must state them.

Ask. "What is the first thing you will place on the plan?" (Best answer: fixed commitments.)

Set-up. Groups of three to four. Hand out blank weekly sheets (or use the template on the next slide). Remind them to include rest.

Say. Read the first statement slowly: "Your prefect badge is temporary. Your character is not." Pause. Then the supporting message. Then, after the question appears, wait in silence for at least ten seconds.

Ask. The final question, once, calmly: "When you leave this school one day and no longer wear your prefect badge, what do you want people to remember about the way you led them?" Do not call for answers. Let them think.

Close. Thank the prefect board, the teachers and the principal. Remind them of their commitment sentences and the first board meeting follow-up. Offer to answer questions privately.
